The UN Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) at its midpoint: what moved, what did not

Countries with a comprehensive-assessment policy for older people rose from 13.9% to 54.6% in eight years. Countries with the longitudinal data to track the results: 22.7%.

On 28 September 2026 the UN Secretary-General transmitted the mid-term progress report on the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing (2021–2030) to the 81st session of the General Assembly (A/81/366)[1][2]. WHO prepared it in coordination with the UN Inter-Agency Group on Ageing, with contributions from 13 UN entities, the World Bank Group and civil society. It covers implementation in 2024–2026 and rests on the 2025/2026 mid-point survey, answered by 152 WHO Member States (78.4%). The conclusion is plain: law and policy have moved, but without financing and data the 2030 targets are out of reach.

About the report

  • Mandate: General Assembly resolution 75/131 (December 2020)[3]. WHO reports to the Assembly every three years. This is the second report and the mid-term one; a near-final report is due in 2029[2].
  • Four action areas: ① combating ageism, ② age-friendly environments (AFE), ③ integrated care (ICOPE and related), ④ long-term care (LTC).
  • Phase change: it marks the shift from phase 1, “strengthening global evidence and partnerships” (2021–2025), to phase 2, “accelerating measurable country-level results through investment and stakeholder engagement” (2026–2030)[2].

Life expectancy rose; healthy life expectancy did not keep pace

The population aged 60 and over passed one billion in 2020 and is projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050. By then 34% of the Western Pacific region and 32% of Europe will be older people, while the African and Eastern Mediterranean regions will more than triple their older populations between 2020 and 2050[2].

The gap at age 60 between life expectancy and healthy life expectancy (HALE) widened between 2000 and 2023, from 4.4 to 4.9 years for men and from 5.4 to 6.2 years for women. The added years were not matched by added healthy years[2].

About 71% of excess COVID-19 deaths occurred among people aged 60 and over (2020–2023). Life expectancy and HALE at 60 fell by roughly a year between 2019 and 2021; as of 2023 only women’s life expectancy (22.8 years) had returned to its pre-pandemic level. Women’s HALE (16.6 years), men’s life expectancy (19.6 years) and men’s HALE (14.7 years) had not[2].

How to read it

  • The largest gains are indicator 7, policy on comprehensive assessment of older people (13.9%→54.6%), and indicator 6, age-friendly environments (17.5%→54.6%). The stated intent to assess older people’s function systematically spread almost fourfold in eight years.
  • The only indicator that fell is 3, the national multistakeholder forum. It peaked at 53.1% in 2020 and has come back down to 43.3%.
  • The lowest is indicator 10, longitudinal data (22.7%, 44 countries), which had dropped to 15.5% in 2023 and has only partly recovered. The report names it the single most important data-infrastructure investment before 2030[2].

Progress by action area (2024–2026)

Combating ageism — the WHO Ageism Scale is in use in 19 countries. Following Human Rights Council resolution 58/13, an intergovernmental working group (IGWG) was established to prepare a legally binding international instrument on the human rights of older persons, and held its first substantive session in July 2026[2].

Age-friendly environments — the WHO Global Network for Age-friendly Cities and Communities (GNAFCC) grew from 1,500 members in 2023 to more than 1,800, across over 60 countries and covering 320 million people. Thika, Kenya became the first African city to join[2].

Integrated care — the second edition of the WHO ICOPE handbook was published in 2024[4]. France became the first high-income country to commit to a nationwide ICOPE roll-out in 2025, and Indonesia introduced annual screening for declines in intrinsic capacity into its national health system from 2025. Costa Rica is applying ICOPE nationally through a social-health model linking day-care centres to primary-care referral, and eight countries in the Eastern Mediterranean region (Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Pakistan, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates) have adopted it. ICOPE interventions are being incorporated into the WHO UHC Service Package platform[2].

Long-term care — WHO published a long-term care package for UHC in 2024 and released draft global standards for long-term care for consultation at the Seventy-ninth World Health Assembly in May 2026. Only 16% of low-income countries have any support scheme for informal carers[2].

The financing gap is this Decade’s binding constraint

Of roughly 145 countries reporting on resources, those describing them as absent or very limited were 43.4% (63 countries) for combating ageism, 37.2% (54) for age-friendly environments, 33.8% (49) for long-term care and 21.4% (31) for integrated care[2]. Legislation guaranteeing access to assistive technology — hearing aids, glasses, walking frames — reaches only 45.4%. WHO reads the lower shortfall reported for ICOPE as a sign that primary care is organised to support integrated care for older people to some degree in about 58% of responding countries[2].

The report also puts economic figures on the table. Ireland’s Healthy Age Friendly Homes programme estimated savings of €90,376 a year where it supports a couple to live independently, and €6,080–12,160 for each fall-related hospital admission avoided[2]. The World Bank states that countries investing early in integrated and long-term care see savings several times the initial outlay[1].

The report read through functional assessment

The report defines healthy ageing as the process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables well-being in older age. Three passages stand out from that angle.

  1. Chronological age is not a proxy for function. WHO analysed comparable data on 151,718 people aged 60 and over in 42 countries and found that about 14% of the world’s older people — more than 142 million — could not meet their basic needs in their own environment, with wide heterogeneity within the same age by sex, education and context. Policy, the report states, cannot be designed as though everyone of a given age had similar needs and capacities[2].
  2. Without longitudinal data there is no way to demonstrate impact. Only 22.7% of countries hold the nationally representative longitudinal data needed to show population-level results. Monitoring leans heavily on Health and Retirement-type studies in about 50 countries, and WHO has launched a new survey programme, WHO-GAPS (Global Ageing Population Survey), in Brunei Darussalam, Kuwait, Poland and Qatar to fill the gap[2].
  3. Countries running ICOPE pathways reported better intrinsic-capacity assessment coverage. The report records improvements in assessment coverage, care-pathway coordination and medication management among countries implementing ICOPE-aligned integrated care, without giving per-country effect sizes[2].

Targets and recommendations for 2028 and 2030

By 2028 (for the 2029 report)

  • More than 60% of Member States with national strategies and plans updated for phase 2
  • Countries with longitudinal studies up from 22.7% to 35%
  • A 25% increase in countries reporting adequate or substantial resources across the four areas
  • Survey response rate up from 78.4% to 90%[2]

By 2030

  • ICOPE-aligned care pathways embedded at scale in primary care in a substantial number of countries
  • Long-term care systems operating with financing in 70% of countries
  • A legally binding instrument on the human rights of older persons drafted or close to it[2]

An AndanteFit reading: the gap between policy and measurement

A/81/366 covers no assessment device or product. But read its indicator structure as a set of practical conditions for measuring physical function, and one pair of numbers stands out. 54.6% of countries have a policy on comprehensive assessment of older people; 22.7% have the longitudinal data to track what that assessment found. Fewer than half the countries that decided to assess are accumulating the results.

That gap looks less like a problem of intent than of method. Recording a single assessment and re-measuring the same person comparably some years later are different requirements. The second needs at least three things.

  • A fixed instrument. A different scale at each measurement does not make a time series.
  • Values that hold when the rater changes. In longitudinal comparison, inter-rater variation becomes measurement error.
  • A low cost of repeating. If a whole population has to be re-measured annually, time and staffing per person decide whether the programme survives.

The first condition is already met. In the WHO ICOPE pathway the report builds on, the SPPB is the step-2 in-depth locomotor capacity assessment that follows step-1 screening with the five-times chair rise, and it carries an internationally comparable 0–12 scale. The same ruler across countries, and across a decade.

That leaves the second and the third. Manual SPPB requires raters trained in the standardised protocol, a point the validation studies of automated SPPB have consistently given as their rationale[5][6]. AndanteFit is built to lower exactly those two axes — inter-rater variation and time per participant.

One of the report’s 2028 targets is to raise the share of countries with longitudinal studies from 22.7% to 35%. Those 12 percentage points are not the kind of number a few new research cohorts fill. It is closer to the kind of target met when measurements taken in the ordinary work of primary care and community programmes remain comparable afterwards — when the physical-function values a community frailty-prevention programme accumulates year after year sit on a standard scale, they are longitudinal data already.

AndanteFit automates the balance, 4 m gait speed and five-times chair stand subtests to deliver an SPPB in under 3 minutes.
