Recovery after cholecystectomy: look at physical function, not just blood tests — biomarker abnormalities and 12-week SPPB

A team from the Departments of Surgery and Rehabilitation Medicine at Kyung Hee University Hospital at Gangdong (Seoul, Korea) analysed 184 adults who received outpatient rehabilitation after cholecystectomy and published the results in Antioxidants[1]. Patients with more abnormal nutritional, inflammatory and redox-related blood tests at their first rehabilitation visit had lower physical function 12 weeks later. The team used the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) as the main measure of recovery.

After surgery, how much everyday function comes back?

Cholecystectomy usually goes smoothly, but not every patient returns to daily life at the same pace. Some have persistent loss of physical function or pain and are referred for outpatient rehabilitation.

In these patients, the researchers asked how blood-test results at the start of rehabilitation related to later physical function. Rather than whether the operation itself succeeded, they focused on how well patients regained the ability to walk, keep their balance and rise from a chair.

This was a single-centre retrospective cohort study of medical records from adults seen in the rehabilitation outpatient clinic after cholecystectomy between 2017 and 2024. It included 184 patients who had their first rehabilitation visit 14–180 days after surgery and completed a reassessment about 12 weeks later. Mean age was 63.2 years, and the median time from surgery to the first rehabilitation visit was 66 days[1].

Counting abnormalities across five blood tests

The team reviewed five blood tests drawn at the first rehabilitation visit. Each test meeting its threshold counted as one abnormality, giving a burden score from 0 to 5.

Together, the five tests reflect nutritional status, inflammation and redox-related physiology. The abnormality count should not be read as a direct measure of the degree of malnutrition or of oxidative stress itself.

More abnormalities, lower SPPB at 12 weeks

The SPPB assesses lower-extremity function through balance, gait speed and repeated chair stands. Scores range from 0 to 12, with higher scores indicating better performance.

Patients with more abnormalities already had lower physical function at the start of rehabilitation and also differed in age and comorbidities. To account for this, the team adjusted for baseline SPPB, pain, age, sex, body mass index, time since surgery, diabetes and comorbidity burden.

After adjustment, each additional abnormality was associated with a 0.49-point lower SPPB at 12 weeks (95% CI −0.80 to −0.18, p = 0.002)[1].

This does not mean that a given patient's SPPB falls by 0.49 points for each added abnormality. It is an average difference between patients after accounting for the other main factors.

Similar associations for grip strength and walking distance

Secondary analyses showed the same pattern: more abnormalities went with weaker grip strength and shorter six-minute walking distance at 12 weeks.

Each additional abnormality was associated with 1.02 kg lower grip strength and a 19.2 m shorter six-minute walk distance at 12 weeks, as well as lower odds of improving SPPB by 2 points or more from baseline[1].

These secondary analyses were exploratory and not adjusted for multiple comparisons. They are best read as supporting the primary finding on 12-week SPPB.

Blood tests add information to functional assessment — they are not a prediction tool

The abnormality count remained associated with 12-week SPPB after accounting for baseline physical function and clinical information. But adding it to a model of existing clinical variables increased explained variance only modestly, by 0.03 in R²[1].

The authors do not present it as a validated tool for predicting an individual's recovery or for choosing treatment. Blood tests may add some information about a patient's vulnerability, but physical function still needs to be assessed directly.

How to read the findings

The study analysed patients who received outpatient rehabilitation after surgery, not all cholecystectomy patients. The results cannot be applied directly to every patient who has the operation.

The frequency, intensity and content of rehabilitation, postoperative complications and dietary intake were not fully analysed. These factors may have influenced both the blood-test results and functional recovery.

As an observational study, it does not establish that blood-test abnormalities cause poorer functional recovery. Nor can it be concluded that correcting values with zinc or vitamin D supplementation improves physical function[1].

What it means for rehabilitation clinics

The authors suggest that, in patients with many abnormalities, this information can prompt a closer look at nutritional status, inflammation and baseline physical function, and inform a review of rehabilitation goals and follow-up plans[1].

In this study, the SPPB was the main tool for capturing function at the start of rehabilitation and for checking status and change at 12 weeks. Where the blood tests described physiological state, the SPPB showed what patients could actually do: walk, keep their balance and rise from a chair.

Understanding recovery after surgery means checking what patients can actually do alongside their lab values. This study illustrates the value of looking at blood tests together with repeated physical function assessment.
