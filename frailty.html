Concepts

Frailtywhat it is, how it is assessed, and whether it can be reversed

Frailty is not the ordinary wear of getting older. It is a state in which physiological reserve and the capacity to recover have both declined, so that a small stressor is enough to send function over a cliff. Two people with the same pneumonia part ways exactly there: one is back on their feet in a week, the other never walks the same way again.

What matters clinically is that frailty is not a fixed state. It can progress and it can be reversed. That makes it less like a diagnosis you either carry or do not, and more like something to be measured repeatedly so you can see which way it is moving.

Definition

What is frailty?

Frailty is a simultaneous decline in the reserve of several physiological systems, which raises vulnerability to outside stressors. The consequence is a higher risk of functional decline, falls, hospitalisation, dependency and death. It is not explained by any single organ or single disease: it is a multidimensional syndrome.

It overlaps with ageing, disease and disability without being any of them. Advanced age does not make someone frail, a person can be frail with no diagnosed disease, and frailty is usually already under way before disability appears. That is exactly where its clinical value lies: it marks the window in which intervention is still possible.

Prefrailty is the intermediate stage where some criteria are met but not all. It is where intervention pays off most, and the usual target of community prevention programmes.
Assessment models

Two ways of measuring frailty

Depending on which one is used, the same word “frailty” refers to different things.

Both models share one axis: physical performance. Three of the phenotype’s five criteria — weakness, slow gait and physical activity — are performance items, and in the deficit model the mobility items carry disproportionate prognostic weight. Whichever model is used, how physical function is measured decides how much the result can be trusted.
Reversibility

Can frailty be reversed?

Yes, with conditions: it depends on how early it is detected and how multicomponent the intervention is.

In a community intervention trial that defined success as improving by at least one Fried phenotype category or gaining at least one SPPB point, 70.4% of the intervention group reached it at 12 weeks against 49.5% of controls, and 81.7% against 51.9% at 32 weeks. Roughly half improve with no intervention at all; with one, the proportion rises clearly and the gap widens over time.

There is cost evidence too. A multicomponent intervention combining exercise, nutrition, depression management, medication review and falls-risk reduction showed, on extended follow-up, fewer hospitalisations and longer time spent living at home.

It is worth reading the evidence in the other direction as well: people who accumulate deficits faster have worse outcomes, according to large longitudinal analyses. So it is not only the current level that matters but the rate of change — another reason a single assessment is not enough.

In short: frailty can be reversed, above all by intervening in a multicomponent way during prefrailty. And to know whether it is being reversed, you have to measure repeatedly on the same scale.
Where measurement fits

What a questionnaire cannot answer

The first step is usually a questionnaire. Self-report tools such as the FRAIL scale let you screen a lot of people quickly, and for that they are well suited.

But a questionnaire captures a perception. The answer to “do you have difficulty walking?” does not always match measured gait speed: some people consider themselves fine and have already lost function, and the reverse happens too. Above all, a questionnaire score cannot show, on the same scale, how much someone improved before and after an intervention.

That is why screening is followed by measurement. Measuring means watching a person do something specific under standardised conditions: the result does not depend on how they describe their own ability, and it can be repeated later and compared directly.

Physical performance measurement

What does the SPPB measure about frailty?

The SPPB does not diagnose frailty. It turns the axis both models share into a repeatable number.

The SPPB scores balance, gait speed and chair stand from 0 to 4 each, for a total of 0 to 12. It directly measures the territory that the phenotype calls weakness and slow gait, and it supplies the basis for the mobility items in the deficit model. The WHO ICOPE framework names it as an assessment tool for the locomotor capacity domain.

Beyond the total, it is worth reading the profile across the three subtests: an isolated balance deficit points somewhere different from a disproportionate chair-stand time. Interpretation and cutoffs are covered in what the SPPB is, and the procedure in how to perform the SPPB test.

That the trial cited above used “one Fried category or one SPPB point” as its endpoint illustrates the relationship well: the frailty category can sit still while the SPPB still registers a one-point change.
Workflow

From screening to reassessment

1

Screening

A questionnaire broadly delimits the at-risk population.

2

Measurement

Physical performance is measured under standardised conditions and recorded from 0 to 12.

3

Intervention

Exercise and nutrition, guided by the score and the subtest profile.

4

Reassessment

Measured again under the same conditions. A one-point change already means something.

If steps 2 and 4 are not carried out under the same conditions, the effect of step 3 cannot be verified. Change the assessor, the chair height, the walk distance or the time of day, and a one-point change disappears into measurement variability.
